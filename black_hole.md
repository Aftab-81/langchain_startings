**Summary – Black Hole – A Comprehensive Report (Prepared 8 Nov 2026)**  

Black holes are extreme spacetime regions whose gravity prevents anything—including light—from escaping once it crosses the event horizon. Over the past century they have evolved from a theoretical curiosity to a cornerstone of astrophysics, cosmology, and fundamental physics. The report reviews the state of knowledge up to 2024‑2025, covering how black holes form, how they are classified, their observable signatures, and the major unanswered questions that drive current research.

**Key Historical Milestones**  
- **1915‑1916:** Einstein’s General Relativity and Schwarzschild’s first exact solution describe static black holes.  
- **1963‑1974:** Kerr introduces rotating solutions; Hawking predicts quantum radiation.  
- **1970s:** X‑ray binaries and quasars provide the first indirect evidence of stellar‑mass black holes (e.g., Cygnus X‑1).  
- **2015:** LIGO’s detection of GW150914 confirms binary black‑hole mergers via gravitational waves.  
- **2019:** The Event Horizon Telescope captures the shadow of M87*, delivering the first direct image of a black‑hole horizon.  
- **2023‑2025:** Advances in X‑ray reflection spectroscopy and very‑long‑baseline interferometry yield high‑precision spin and mass measurements for a growing sample of black holes.

**Formation & Classification**  
- **Stellar‑mass black holes (≈5–100 M☉):** Collapse of massive stars after supernovae, sometimes observed in X‑ray binaries.  
- **Intermediate‑mass black holes (10²–10⁵ M☉):** Likely formed via runaway stellar collisions in dense clusters; still rare and under active search.  
- **Supermassive black holes (10⁶–10¹⁰ M☉):** Reside in galaxy centers; grow through gas accretion, mergers, and possibly direct collapse of primordial gas clouds.
